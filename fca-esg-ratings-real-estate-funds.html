What the FCA's ESG Ratings Regime Means for Real Estate Funds

PS26/22 regulates how ESG ratings are produced and communicated. For real estate funds, this changes the rules around the information ecosystem that shapes how investors, lenders and allocators assess property portfolios.

On 3 November 2026, the FCA published PS26/22, finalising the UK's first regulatory regime for ESG ratings providers. From 29 June 2028, any firm providing ESG ratings in the UK must hold FCA authorisation.

The regime regulates how ESG ratings are produced, governed and communicated. It does not prescribe a particular sustainability methodology. For real estate funds, this is not a compliance obligation to prepare for. Rather, it changes the rules around the information ecosystem that increasingly shapes how investors, lenders and allocators assess property portfolios. This briefing explains what shifted and what it means for fund-level ESG strategy.

1. What the regime requires of ESG ratings providers

PS26/22 introduces a conduct framework built on five pillars: transparency, governance, systems and controls, conflicts of interest, and stakeholder engagement.

Transparency. The FCA has established a tiered disclosure framework. Providers must make public disclosures covering methodology, objectives, coverage, data approach and limitations. Direct users of ratings receive additional product-level and rating-level information. Rated entities can request detailed disclosures on how their rating was produced. Providers may rely on a trade-secrets exemption where its conditions are met, so the regime does not require every proprietary input to be published, but it does require that ratings are understandable.

Governance and systems. Providers must have clear UK accountability, though not every rating function or senior employee must be UK-based. Outsourcing is permitted but the authorised firm retains responsibility for the ratings it provides in the UK. Providers will be Core firms under the Senior Managers and Certification Regime (SM&CR), meaning approved senior managers, conduct rules and fitness-and-propriety requirements apply.

Conflicts of interest. Providers must identify, prevent and manage conflicts of interest. Public disclosure of conflicts is treated as a last resort, used only when material risks to rating integrity or independence remain after management steps.

Stakeholder engagement. Rated entities must receive at least two business days' notice before a new rating is first made available. They can request the data underlying a rating and offer factual corrections, though providers need not wait for feedback before publication. Direct users must receive advance notice of material methodology changes.

Authorisation. Applications are staggered by projected UK revenue. Providers expecting £500,000 or more in UK ESG rating revenue must apply between 24 June and 29 July 2027. Those below that threshold apply between 30 July and 31 August 2027. Carrying on in-scope activity without authorisation after 29 June 2028 will breach the general prohibition under FSMA and constitute a criminal offence.

Notably, the Consumer Duty does not apply to ESG rating activities, nor do the Financial Ombudsman Service or FSCS protections. No bespoke prudential requirements are imposed.

2. What changes for real estate funds as users of ESG ratings

Real estate funds use ESG ratings in several ways: to inform portfolio construction, to satisfy LP reporting requirements, to benchmark against peers, and increasingly to meet sustainability-linked loan covenants. The regulated ratings market should improve all of these use cases, but in ways that require funds to be more active participants rather than passive consumers.

Methodology transparency will expose gaps. When providers publish how their methodologies work, including data sources, weighting approaches and materiality frameworks, fund teams will be able to see for the first time exactly what data feeds their scores. For many real estate funds, this will reveal that the data flowing from their portfolio to the ratings provider is incomplete, inconsistent, or not aligned with what the methodology actually rewards. A fund that scores well on governance but poorly on environmental performance may discover that the environmental score reflects missing data rather than poor performance.

Advance notice of methodology changes. Direct users will receive notice before material methodology changes take effect. This is a significant shift from the current situation, where score movements often arrive without explanation. Fund teams will need processes to assess how methodology changes affect their portfolio scores and, where relevant, their sustainability-linked loan covenants.

Comparability should improve, but slowly. The regime does not mandate a single methodology. Methodological diversity is explicitly preserved as a feature of the market. Funds comparing GRESB scores, MSCI ratings and Sustainalytics assessments will still face different frameworks. What should improve is the ability to understand why ratings differ, since each provider must now explain its approach publicly.

3. What changes for real estate funds as rated entities

Many real estate funds and REITs are themselves the subjects of ESG ratings. Under PS26/22, rated entities gain new rights that create both opportunities and obligations.

Notification before first rating. Rated entities must receive at least two business days' notice before a new rating is first published. This is a procedural right, not a veto. Providers do not need to wait for feedback before making the rating available. But it does mean that a fund hearing about a new ESG rating for the first time via a Bloomberg terminal alert or an LP enquiry should become a thing of the past.

Right to request underlying data. Rated entities can request the data that was used to produce their rating. This is perhaps the most consequential change for real estate funds. The ability to see what data the provider used, and to offer factual corrections, means fund teams can identify where missing or outdated information is dragging down scores. The practical question is whether funds have the internal documentation to take advantage of this right. A fund that cannot readily produce the evidence behind its ESG claims will struggle to mount an effective factual correction.

No advance notice of methodology changes. Unlike direct users, rated entities will not receive advance notice of methodology changes. This was a deliberate choice by the FCA. It means that funds relying on a particular rating for covenant compliance or LP reporting cannot assume they will see scoring changes coming.

4. The missing piece: asset-level disclosure quality

PS26/22 is a significant step. But it regulates the intermediaries, not the information supply chain that feeds them.

ESG ratings for real estate are only as good as the data flowing from individual assets into portfolio-level reporting and then into the ratings providers' models. That data pipeline remains unregulated and, in practice, inconsistent. Plinthos Institute research on UK-listed REITs found a 72-point gap in disclosure quality between the strongest and weakest reporters when assessed against 120 ESG data points (Xu, 2026, SSRN). The gap is not primarily about ESG performance: companies making genuine sustainability investments often fail to surface that work in formats that ratings providers can use.

The new regime will make this problem more visible, not less. When methodology transparency reveals what data a rating is built on, funds with poor disclosure infrastructure will see clearly that their scores reflect data gaps rather than genuine underperformance. The regime gives rated entities the right to request underlying data and offer corrections, but exercising that right requires having well-documented, evidence-based ESG narratives ready to deploy.

The core challenge: regulating the outputs (ratings) is necessary. But the transformation in how capital markets price sustainability in property will come from improving the inputs: structured, verifiable, asset-level ESG documentation that makes a fund's sustainability investment visible to every intermediary in the chain.

5. Timeline and action points

Date Event
3 November 2026 FCA publishes PS26/22: final rules for ESG ratings regime
February 2027 FCA Pre-Application Support Service (PASS) opens for providers
24 June 2027 Authorisation gateway opens (larger providers first)
30 July 2027 Second application window opens (smaller providers)
31 August 2027 Application window closes
29 June 2028 Regime goes live: authorisation required

For real estate fund sustainability teams, the practical actions are:

  1. Audit your disclosure pipeline. Map how ESG data flows from asset level through to portfolio reporting and into the ratings providers you use. Identify where data is missing, inconsistent, or not aligned with provider methodologies.
  2. Prepare to engage with published methodologies. When providers publish their transparency disclosures, review them against your portfolio data. Identify the specific data points where your fund's disclosure falls short of what the methodology rewards.
  3. Build your factual correction capability. The right to request underlying data and offer factual corrections is only useful if you have documented, evidence-based ESG narratives ready. Ensure that sustainability claims at the asset level are traceable and auditable.
  4. Review SLL and LP reporting dependencies. If your fund's sustainability-linked loan covenants or LP reporting reference specific ESG ratings, assess the risk that methodology changes could trigger covenant issues. The regime requires advance notice of methodology changes to direct users, but not to rated entities.
